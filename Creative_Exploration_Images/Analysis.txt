================================================================================
CREATIVE EXPLORATION — CORAL BLOB DETECTOR
Analysis of Design Decisions
================================================================================

MOTIVATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
After completing the Hough Transform pipeline, we were dissatisfied with its
output. The Hough Circle Transform is a geometric shape detector — it only
finds perfect circles. Coral fragments are organic, irregularly-shaped, and
branching. No circle accurately describes their boundary, and the mesh grid in
the coral nursery image produced clean circular arc segments (from the rounded
mesh-cell corners) that the accumulator mistook for coral. We needed a different
approach that did not assume any particular shape.

Our new pipeline asks a simpler question: rather than "does this collection of
edge pixels trace a circle?", we ask: "Is this cluster of dark pixels the right
color, the right size, and the right shape to be a coral fragment?"

================================================================================
STAGE 1 — COLOR THRESHOLDING  (isCoralPixel)
================================================================================

Why we included it:
    The coral nursery image has a clear visual pattern: the coral fragments
    are noticeably darker than the water background and lighter mesh. They also
    have a warm brownish cast. This color signature is a far more direct discriminator 
    than edge shape, and so we skip edge detection entirely for this pipeline.

What it does:
    Converts each pixel to grayscale average (r+g+b)/3 and accepts it as a
    coral candidate if:
        (a) gray < 130  — it is dark enough to be coral
        (b) r >= g >= b — the slight warm/brown tint is present
        (c) saturation (max - min channel) <= 60 — excludes the brightly
            colored identification tags attached to some coral pieces

Why each condition:
    - gray < 130: the mesh wires and water background are noticeably brighter;
      130 was the threshold that captured coral body pixels while rejecting
      most background.
    - r >= g >= b: coral has more red than green and more green than blue,
      producing the warm tone. Pure grays (shadows, mesh shadows) satisfy
      gray < 130 but not this ordering, which helps reject them.
    - saturation <= 60: the identification tag next to the coral pieces is
      highly saturated (bright orange/yellow). Without this guard, the tag
      would be included as coral.

Output: a binary mask where 255 = candidate coral pixel, 0 = background.

================================================================================
STAGE 2 — MORPHOLOGICAL CLOSING  (dilate × 4, then erode × 4)
================================================================================

Why we included it:
    The color threshold alone produces a broken mask. A single coral fragment
    is not uniformly dark, as there highlights, internal texture variation,
    and partial mesh shadow all create small gaps of non-coral pixels within
    what is visually one piece of coral. Without closing these gaps, BFS in
    the next stage would split one coral piece into many small disconnected
    blobs, each too small to pass the area filter.

What closing does:
    Dilation (expand) first: grows every confirmed coral pixel outward by
    one pixel in all 8 directions. Four rounds of this fills gaps up to
    ~4 pixels wide, bridging the interior holes within a coral fragment.

    Erosion (shrink) after: shrinks the expanded mask back inward by the
    same amount. This restores the approximate original blob boundary while
    keeping the internal gaps filled. Structures that were connected only
    because of the dilation remain connected after erosion.

The trade-off we accepted:
    Four dilation rounds expand the mask 4 pixels in every direction. If a
    coral pixel and a mesh strand pixel are within 4 pixels of each other,
    dilation bridges them and they become one blob. The subsequent erosion
    does not separate them. This is why some detected blobs may contain
    both coral and adjacent mesh material. A smaller dilation radius (1–2
    rounds) would reduce this fusion but would leave more internal coral
    gaps unfilled.

    Additionally, at mesh intersection nodes — where two strands cross and
    form a squarish dark patch — closing can produce a blob that looks
    locally similar to a small coral fragment. The density and aspect filter
    in Stage 4 is our primary way to mitigate this tradeoff.

================================================================================
STAGE 3 — CONNECTED COMPONENTS VIA BFS
================================================================================

Why we included it:
    After morphological closing, the binary mask contains many isolated
    regions of white pixels. We need to group them into discrete blobs so
    that each blob can be evaluated and either accepted or rejected as a
    coral piece. BFS (breadth-first search) is the natural algorithm for
    this: start at any unvisited white pixel, flood-fill all 8-connected
    neighbors, and record the full extent of the region.

What it tracks per blob:
    - area: total pixel count — used in the area filter (Stage 5)
    - minX, maxX, minY, maxY: bounding box corners — used to compute the
      density and aspect ratio in Stage 4, and to draw the output rectangle

Why 8-connectivity instead of 4:
    Coral fragments are organic and diagonal connections are common. Using
    only 4-connectivity (up/down/left/right) would incorrectly split blobs
    that touch diagonally, producing more fragments than actual coral pieces.

================================================================================
STAGE 4 — DENSITY AND ASPECT RATIO FILTER
================================================================================

Why we included it:
    Even after color thresholding and morphological closing, the binary mask
    still contains blobs from mesh strands and intersection nodes. The mesh's
    color passes the warm-dark threshold in some lighting conditions. We
    needed a shape-based filter that exploits the structural difference
    between coral (compact, blobby) and mesh (thin, elongated strands).

Density:
    density = blob.area / (bounding_box_width × bounding_box_height)

    A compact coral fragment fills most of its bounding box → density ≈ 1.0.
    A thin mesh strand spans a large bounding box but leaves most of it
    empty → density ≈ 0.05–0.15.

Aspect ratio:
    aspect = bounding_box_width / bounding_box_height

    A roughly blobby coral piece has aspect near 1.0. A horizontal mesh
    strand is much wider than tall → aspect > 3.0. A vertical mesh strand
    is much taller than wide → aspect < 0.5 (i.e., 1/aspect > 3.0 in that
    direction).

The filter logic:
    Discard a blob only if BOTH conditions fail simultaneously:
        density < 0.5  AND  (aspect > 3.0 OR aspect < 0.5)

    This is intentionally conservative. A blob is kept if it is either
    dense enough OR not elongated enough. This prevents the filter from
    accidentally discarding small but legitimate coral fragments that happen
    to have an elongated bounding box (e.g., a branching arm of coral).
    The cost is that squarish mesh nodes — which are dense-ish and not
    elongated — can still pass. The area filter in Stage 5 is a second
    line of defense against those.

================================================================================
STAGE 5 — AREA FILTER  (minArea / maxArea)
================================================================================

Why we included it:
    Two categories of false positives survive Stage 4:
        1. Very small blobs: isolated noise pixels, tiny mesh shadow patches,
           or single-pixel artifacts from the color threshold. These have
           areas too small to be a real coral fragment.
        2. Very large blobs: regions where morphological closing fused
           multiple coral pieces (or coral + mesh) into one giant connected
           component. These are too large to be a single fragment.

    A two-sided area gate [minArea, maxArea] removes both.

How parameters were chosen (from Results.txt):
    Run #1 used minArea=50, maxArea=10000. This captured fragments ranging
    from small pieces (~50–200 px²) up to large clusters (~2000–2500 px²).
    Subsequent runs tuned these bounds based on visual inspection of which
    detections were genuine coral vs. noise or mesh artifacts.

================================================================================
STAGE 6 — OUTPUT DRAWING
================================================================================

Why rectangles instead of circles:
    The Hough pipeline drew circles — which were wrong for irregular coral
    shapes. We replaced circles with axis-aligned bounding rectangles plus
    a center dot. The rectangle communicates the actual spatial extent of
    the detected blob without implying any particular geometric shape,
    which is more honest about what the algorithm actually found.

================================================================================
SUMMARY OF DESIGN RATIONALE
================================================================================

  Stage         Solves
  ------------- -----------------------------------------------------------------
  Color thresh  Removes background and water; targets coral's dark warm tone
  Sat. guard    Excludes brightly colored identification tags on coral pieces
  Closing       Heals interior gaps so one coral fragment registers as one blob
  BFS           Groups pixels into discrete, evaluable regions
  Density       Rejects thin, elongated mesh strands
  Aspect        Reinforces density: catches elongated blobs even if moderately dense
  Area filter   Removes noise (too small) and fused mega-blobs (too large)
  Rectangles    Honest representation of irregular blob extent
